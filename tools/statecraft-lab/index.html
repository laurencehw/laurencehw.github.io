Statecraft Lab

What an economic sanction costs its target on the first day, how fast that cost wears off, and how fifteen real cases turned out.

A teaching tool, and the companion to Economic Coercion. The first part is the arithmetic of that book's model, the second a stylized picture of its race between clocks, and the third the fifteen cases the book codes, from Jefferson's embargo to the chip controls.

1. What a denial costs today

A coercer supplies some share of what the target uses, withholds part of it, and part of what it withholds comes back through third countries. What is left is the net withdrawal. How much that hurts depends on how far the target's market can adjust.

2. The race between clocks

The coercer wins if the target's government gives way before its economy adapts. Here the shortage from part 1 shrinks as the target replaces it, and the pressure on the government is the price rise on what is still missing, scaled by how much of that burden reaches anyone who decides. The government concedes only while pressure is above its threshold.

This part is a drawing of the argument, not an estimate. The decay of the shortage and the weighting are my simplest choices of form, and the elasticity is held at its short-run value so that pressure falls only as supply is replaced. No one has measured a government's concession threshold.

3. Fifteen cases

The cases are the ones Economic Coercion works through, coded by me from its chapters. Each is placed by the months it took the target to replace most of what was denied. A filled dot means it had adapted by then. An open dot with an arrow means it had not adapted when the record stops, so the true figure is longer. Choose a case to read it.

What this leaves out

The exposure arithmetic is written for the denial of an input. A price cap, a reserve freeze and a ban on network access each work differently, and the book treats them in their own chapters. The pressure path has no network clock: it cannot show a sanction pushing the target's partners onto a rival payment system or standard that outlives the dispute. Leakage is held fixed, though in practice it grows with the price gap and shrinks with enforcement.

The fifteen cases are not a sample. I chose them because they are well documented and famous, which favours long, large episodes against targets able to resist. The clock, exposure band, concession and verdict in each row are my judgments, and the months are taken or derived from dated statements in the chapters. The counts describe the book's cases and support no causal claim.

Read with

Economic Coercion: Chapter 1 for the model, Appendix B for the arithmetic of part 1 and an exercise on the case file, and Chapter 9 for the historical cases and the places where the model fails. For the same ideas played as decisions, see EconWar.

Method: net withdrawal is share times fraction withheld times one minus leakage. The price rise is the exact constant-elasticity form, one minus the withdrawal raised to the power of minus one over the elasticity, less one; the rule of thumb is the withdrawal divided by the elasticity. In part 2 the replaceable shortage halves at the chosen interval.
