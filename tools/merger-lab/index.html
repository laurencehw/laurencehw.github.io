Merger Lab

Three screens an economist runs on a horizontal merger, in the order a review runs them: concentration, pricing pressure, and a simulated new equilibrium. Change any number and all three update.

A teaching tool, and the companion to the merger chapters of Antitrust Research Methods. It opens on that book's four-product example, and its simulation follows the book's own code step for step. The numbers in the example are invented.

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index is the sum of squared firm shares, in percent, of sales inside the market. The 2023 US Merger Guidelines presume harm when the index ends above 1,800 and rises by more than 100, or when the merged firm's share exceeds 30 percent with the same rise. It is a presumption that evidence can rebut.

2. Pricing pressure

After the merger, some of the customers a product loses when its price rises go to its new partner, and the merged firm keeps the margin on them. Upward pricing pressure values that recapture: diversion to the partner's products times the margin earned there, less any saving in cost.

Diversion here is what logit demand implies: lost customers spread across the other products, and the outside option, in proportion to share. GUPPI is the pressure before efficiencies, as a share of price. The first-order price rise multiplies the pressure by the pass-through rate; one half is the rule of thumb for linear demand.

3. Simulation

The screens above hold everyone else's prices fixed. The simulation does not. It fits a logit demand system to the shares and margins, then solves for the prices at which no firm, merged or rival, wants to change its price again.

What this leaves out

Logit demand forces diversion to follow share, so the model cannot say that two products are unusually close substitutes; a real review would estimate diversion from switching data, surveys or a richer demand model. The outside share is an assumption, and the results move with it: try 10 percent and then 40. Margins are taken as true marginal-cost margins, which accounting margins rarely are. Nothing here allows for entry, repositioning, buyer power or coordination among the firms that remain, and the efficiency is whatever you assume it to be.

The book's merger chapter runs the same steps on real data, a simulated Chrysler and AMC merger in the 1986 US car market, with a demand model in which substitution depends on the cars' characteristics. There the simulated price rise for the acquired firm's best-selling car is about three times the first-order figure.

Read with

Antitrust Research Methods: the chapter on the industrial organization toolkit for upward pricing pressure, and the chapter on mergers for the simulation, the guidelines and the South African cases.

Method: Bertrand competition with multiproduct firms and logit demand. The price coefficient is calibrated from the observed margins through the first-order conditions, marginal costs are those the calibrated model implies at observed prices, and post-merger prices are found by damped fixed-point iteration. With no efficiency the calculation is the one in the book's run_logit_sim(). Consumer loss is the change in the logit inclusive value, in price units per potential buyer.
