Reading paths

The books were written to be read together, and a course rarely needs only one of them. This page sets out eight paths through the collection and the five threads that connect it. For the books one at a time, see the full list.

Paths and course bundles

These are the combinations I would use, in the order I would use them. Books still marked in progress appear as supplements, never as the spine of a course.

A first graduate year, read institutionally
For a master's or PhD student who wants a second view beside the standard core sequence, not a replacement for it.
  1. Six Cases, Five Lenses, then Institutional Microeconomics alongside the core micro course.
  2. The macro argument, then Part I of Institutionalist Macro on measurement, alongside the core macro course.
  3. Empirical Research Methods beside the econometrics sequence.
  4. For a reading group: Philosophy of Economics, Chapter 14, on what a macro aggregate is, read against the macro measurement chapters.
Money and finance
A two-semester sequence built on balance sheets: who owes what to whom, and who can be made to pay when the funding stops.
  1. Institutionalist Macro, Chapter 3 (flow of funds) and Chapter 10 (money and payments), as the bridge from the national accounts.
  2. Global Finance, the first sixteen chapters, as the core semester.
  3. Financial Economics for pricing, capital structure and the investors who hold the claims.
  4. The Financial Crisis as the extended case, read with Macro Chapter 19 on crises in general.
A policy-school core
For an MPP year, or a public finance course that wants the evidence and the rules beside the theory.
  1. Public Economics as the course text.
  2. The minimum-wage, housing and hospital-merger threads of Institutional Microeconomics for the market-failure weeks.
  3. The causal-inference part of Empirical Research Methods for reading the evidence.
  4. The American Economy as a reference on the sectors that programs and regulators reach.
Economic statecraft
For a course on sanctions, export controls and the geopolitics of supply chains.
  1. Economic Coercion, starting with Chapter 1 and its model of two margins and three clocks.
  2. Institutionalist Macro, Chapter 22, and the dealer and central-bank chapters of Global Finance, for why financial sanctions reach as far as the dollar does.
  3. The Chinese Economy, from its argument page, for the other side of most current episodes.
  4. Law and Economics for the doctrine behind the statutes.
Competition and regulation
From the theory of market power to evidence that survives a tribunal.
  1. The platform and hospital-merger threads of Institutional Microeconomics.
  2. Antitrust Research Methods as the course text.
  3. Law and Economics for the legal background a non-lawyer needs.
Development, Africa and South Africa
From the general questions to the economies that test them.
  1. Development Economics, which treats the experimental and the structural traditions as one field.
  2. World Economic History for the long run, and Political Economy for institutions.
  3. The African Economy and The South African Economy, with Regional Economics and its data labs for the comparative frame.
Ideas and method
A reading course for students who want to know where economics came from and what kind of knowledge it produces.
  1. Why questions, not schools, then An Introduction to Economic Thought, with its index of Nobel lectures as primary reading.
  2. Philosophy of Economics.
  3. World Economic History, which teaches identification strategies through the papers that used them.
For a general reader
The New Population Panic and The Financial Crisis need no prerequisites. The argument pages of Institutionalist Macro and The Chinese Economy say in a page what each book claims.

How the books connect

Five commitments run through the collection, and they are what make it one project rather than twenty-two. Each is carried by several books, and each book is easier to read once you see which threads it picks up.

Measurement is knowledge, not housekeeping
The numbers are made by agencies making choices, and the choices move policy. Part I of Institutionalist Macro makes the case for the US accounts; Philosophy of Economics, Chapter 14 asks what an aggregate is; The Chinese Economy's note on sources asks how far official statistics can be trusted; World Economic History pairs every era with how we know what we know.
Balance sheets before behaviour
Crises, monetary policy and the reach of the dollar start from who owes what to whom. The thread runs from Macro's flow of funds and dollar system chapters through Global Finance and the holders in Financial Economics to The Financial Crisis and the financial-sanctions chapter of Economic Coercion.
The rules are part of the economy
Statutes, agencies and program rules are not background. Public Economics works from the actual US rules, Antitrust Research Methods from case law in three jurisdictions, Economic Coercion from the statutory authorities in its legal appendix, and Institutional Microeconomics treats the firm, the contract and the commons as institutions in their own right.
Power and distribution matter
Who gains and who pays is part of the analysis, not an afterword. See Macro's Chapter 25 on inequality, the normative chapters of Public Economics, Political Economy, the race and class thread in The Financial Crisis, the labour chapters of The South African Economy, and the philosophy in The New Population Panic.
Place and history
Economies are particular. The country books, City Economics and Regional Economics work at three scales; Development Economics, World Economic History and Macro's chapter on growth ask how they got there; An Introduction to Economic Thought shows that the big questions were first asked of particular economies too.
