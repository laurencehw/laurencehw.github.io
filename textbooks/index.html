Textbook experiments

Why these exist

These are the books I wish had existed when I was in graduate school. A textbook should be current; it should not cost students an arm and a leg; it should be easy to revise; and it should be readable from the very technical to the less so. Economists should also know more philosophy, history, history of thought, and neighbouring disciplines than the standard curriculum asks of them, and several of these books exist to make that easier.

They are experiments, and they are written with AI. The outline, the argument and the judgment of what matters are mine; the figures are generated from code and public data that anyone can re-run. AI is what lets one teacher write the textbook a course actually needs, keep it current, and fix it when a reader finds a mistake. Illustrative figures are labelled as such, citations are checked against the published record, and the errors that remain are mine. If you find one, please tell me; a corrected chapter can be live the same day.

Some of them are also arguments about how a subject should be taught, which is to say about what in it matters: empirical methods organized around the question rather than the technique; macroeconomics in the manner of the American institutionalists, attentive to what we are measuring and how, the map and the territory; international finance through the money view.

All of them are free. They are living drafts, published as they are written at laurence-wilse-samson.gitbook.io, and they vary in how far along they are, so each carries a status: course-tested (taught from, at least once), first edition (published), revised full draft (complete and through at least one full review), full draft (complete, not yet revised) or in progress. Five are far enough along to teach from now.

Find a book by question

Most readers arrive with a question, not a field. Here is where I would send you.

What do the macro numbers actually measure, and does it change policy?
Institutionalist Macro. Start with its argument on one page: six propositions, each with the chapters that carry it and the evidence that would show it wrong.
How does the international monetary system work, day to day?
Global Finance: A Money View.
How are firms financed and assets priced, and who holds them?
Financial Economics.
What happened in 2008, and who paid for it?
The Financial Crisis; its chapter on why so few mortgages were modified is the place to begin.
What should government tax and spend, and on what principles?
Public Economics.
How do markets, firms and non-market arrangements allocate things?
Institutional Microeconomics, starting with Six Cases, Five Lenses: minimum wages, housing, bank runs, platforms, hospital mergers and fisheries, each seen through every tradition the book uses.
How do I build evidence that a competition authority or a court will accept?
Antitrust Research Methods; for the wider doctrine, Law and Economics.
How do sanctions, export controls and tariffs work, and when do they bite?
Economic Coercion.
How do I design an empirical study?
Empirical Research Methods; for the same methods at work on the long run, World Economic History.
Why are some countries rich and others poor, and what can policy do about it?
Development Economics, then World Economic History and Political Economy.
How does a particular economy actually work?
The American Economy, The Chinese Economy, The South African Economy or The African Economy; below the national scale, City Economics, and across world regions, Regional Economics.
Should we worry about falling birth rates?
The New Population Panic.
Where did these ideas come from, and what kind of knowledge is economics?
An Introduction to Economic Thought and Philosophy of Economics.

Start here

If you are choosing one book to read or to teach from, start with one of these five. Each has exercises with worked answers and instructor materials, and all but the last a course pathway.

Public Economics Revised full draft
For an MPP or advanced undergraduate public finance course. It puts the theory, the causal evidence and the actual rules of US programs side by side, and keeps the normative questions (Rawls, Sen, Nozick) on the page rather than in a preface. It is the text for my NYU Wagner course and is kept current as the rules change, which the standard texts cannot do between editions. Instructors: a fourteen-week syllabus and an MPP variant, a solutions manual and an assessment bank, on request. The Fiscal Policy Calculator scores proposals the way its chapters describe.
Global Finance: A Money View Revised full draft
For an international finance course that wants the plumbing: balance sheets, dealers, the hierarchy of money, and liquidity rather than solvency as the binding constraint, after Perry Mehrling. The first sixteen chapters, from the money view to central bank operations, are the core course; the crisis and periphery chapters extend it. Instructors: four course designs in the instructor's guide (a thirteen-week undergraduate course, a thirteen-week graduate seminar, a seven-week crisis half-course and a five-session practitioner programme), 57 problems and 48 exercises, all with worked solutions, and live-data exercises against BIS and IMF series. Balance of Power is the data companion.
Financial Economics: Claims, Prices, Holders Full draft
For a first graduate or advanced undergraduate finance course that would otherwise be taught from a corporate-finance text and an asset-pricing text that never meet. The claims firms issue, the prices they trade at and the investors who hold them are one subject here, so capital structure, discount rates and the institutional ownership of assets are treated together. Instructors: two thirteen-week syllabus maps, problems in every substantive chapter with selected worked solutions, and the rest on request.
Antitrust Research Methods Course-tested
For a practitioner or graduate course in competition economics that has to produce evidence a tribunal will accept, not only estimates. It sets the econometrics beside case law, qualitative evidence and agency practice across the US, EU and South Africa, with the Southern African cases treated as seriously as the American ones, and every figure reproducible in R. It is the text for the CCRED practitioner course at the University of Johannesburg. Instructors: exercises with worked answers in every chapter, including checkable data exercises, and an empirical appendix that doubles as a methods reference.
Economic Coercion Revised full draft
For a course on economic statecraft that wants the law and the economics in one place: sanctions, export controls, investment screening and tariffs, each traced through the statutes and agencies that make it work and through a simple model of coercion (two margins, three clocks) that predicts the order in which a measure's effects appear, with the target's own view of each episode kept on the page. The field otherwise has journalism and law reviews but few textbooks. Instructors: eleven tabletop exercises of 60 to 90 minutes, one per chapter, and nine analytical problems with worked answers; no syllabus yet, and the legal appendix awaits a specialist's review. Companion to EconWar.

Why these five and not the others: they are the books I teach from or would teach from now, with the apparatus a course needs. Several others have more original arguments and will join this list as their exercises and reviews are completed.

Paths and course bundles

The books were written to be read together, and a course rarely needs only one of them. These are the combinations I would use, in the order I would use them. Books still marked in progress appear as supplements, never as the spine of a course.

A first graduate year, read institutionally
For a master's or PhD student who wants a second view beside the standard core sequence, not a replacement for it.
  1. Six Cases, Five Lenses, then Institutional Microeconomics alongside the core micro course.
  2. The macro argument, then Part I of Institutionalist Macro on measurement, alongside the core macro course.
  3. Empirical Research Methods beside the econometrics sequence.
  4. For a reading group: Philosophy of Economics, Chapter 14, on what a macro aggregate is, read against the macro measurement chapters.
Money and finance
A two-semester sequence built on balance sheets: who owes what to whom, and who can be made to pay when the funding stops.
  1. Institutionalist Macro, Chapter 3 (flow of funds) and Chapter 10 (money and payments), as the bridge from the national accounts.
  2. Global Finance, the first sixteen chapters, as the core semester.
  3. Financial Economics for pricing, capital structure and the investors who hold the claims.
  4. The Financial Crisis as the extended case, read with Macro Chapter 19 on crises in general.
A policy-school core
For an MPP year, or a public finance course that wants the evidence and the rules beside the theory.
  1. Public Economics as the course text.
  2. The minimum-wage, housing and hospital-merger threads of Institutional Microeconomics for the market-failure weeks.
  3. The causal-inference part of Empirical Research Methods for reading the evidence.
  4. The American Economy as a reference on the sectors that programs and regulators reach.
Economic statecraft
For a course on sanctions, export controls and the geopolitics of supply chains.
  1. Economic Coercion, starting with Chapter 1 and its model of two margins and three clocks.
  2. Institutionalist Macro, Chapter 22, and the dealer and central-bank chapters of Global Finance, for why financial sanctions reach as far as the dollar does.
  3. The Chinese Economy, from its argument page, for the other side of most current episodes.
  4. Law and Economics for the doctrine behind the statutes.
Competition and regulation
From the theory of market power to evidence that survives a tribunal.
  1. The platform and hospital-merger threads of Institutional Microeconomics.
  2. Antitrust Research Methods as the course text.
  3. Law and Economics for the legal background a non-lawyer needs.
Development, Africa and South Africa
From the general questions to the economies that test them.
  1. Development Economics, which treats the experimental and the structural traditions as one field.
  2. World Economic History for the long run, and Political Economy for institutions.
  3. The African Economy and The South African Economy, with Regional Economics and its data labs for the comparative frame.
Ideas and method
A reading course for students who want to know where economics came from and what kind of knowledge it produces.
  1. Why questions, not schools, then An Introduction to Economic Thought, with its index of Nobel lectures as primary reading.
  2. Philosophy of Economics.
  3. World Economic History, which teaches identification strategies through the papers that used them.
For a general reader
The New Population Panic and The Financial Crisis need no prerequisites. The argument pages of Institutionalist Macro and The Chinese Economy say in a page what each book claims.

How the books connect

Five commitments run through the collection, and they are what make it one project rather than twenty-two. Each is carried by several books, and each book is easier to read once you see which threads it picks up.

Measurement is knowledge, not housekeeping
The numbers are made by agencies making choices, and the choices move policy. Part I of Institutionalist Macro makes the case for the US accounts; Philosophy of Economics, Chapter 14 asks what an aggregate is; The Chinese Economy's note on sources asks how far official statistics can be trusted; World Economic History pairs every era with how we know what we know.
Balance sheets before behaviour
Crises, monetary policy and the reach of the dollar start from who owes what to whom. The thread runs from Macro's flow of funds and dollar system chapters through Global Finance and the holders in Financial Economics to The Financial Crisis and the financial-sanctions chapter of Economic Coercion.
The rules are part of the economy
Statutes, agencies and program rules are not background. Public Economics works from the actual US rules, Antitrust Research Methods from case law in three jurisdictions, Economic Coercion from the statutory authorities in its legal appendix, and Institutional Microeconomics treats the firm, the contract and the commons as institutions in their own right.
Power and distribution matter
Who gains and who pays is part of the analysis, not an afterword. See Macro's Chapter 25 on inequality, the normative chapters of Public Economics, Political Economy, the race and class thread in The Financial Crisis, the labour chapters of The South African Economy, and the philosophy in The New Population Panic.
Place and history
Economies are particular. The country books, City Economics and Regional Economics work at three scales; Development Economics, World Economic History and Macro's chapter on growth ask how they got there; An Introduction to Economic Thought shows that the big questions were first asked of particular economies too.

All twenty-two, by field

Foundations

  • Institutionalist Macro: An American Approach Revised full draft — macroeconomics in the tradition of the American institutionalists, built around the national accounts and the flow of funds: what we are measuring, and how.
  • Institutional Microeconomics Revised full draft — markets, firms, and non-market arrangements as institutions for allocating resources, from the price mechanism and why firms exist through information, market design, and the commons.
  • Empirical Research Methods Full draft — organized around research questions rather than techniques, with quantitative and qualitative methods as complements, and R and Python code throughout.

Money and finance

  • Global Finance: A Money View Revised full draft — international finance as balance sheets, dealers and a hierarchy of money, after Perry Mehrling, where liquidity rather than solvency is the binding constraint; Balance of Power is its data companion.
  • Financial Economics: Claims, Prices, Holders Full draft — corporate finance and asset pricing taught as one subject: the claims firms issue, the prices they trade at and the investors who hold them, so that capital structure, discount rates and who owns the assets are never separated.
  • The Financial Crisis In progress — 2008 through the plumbing: the institutional arrangements and market devices that let a housing correction cascade into a global crisis, and along what lines its costs fell.

Public economics and law

  • Public Economics Revised full draft — public finance theory, causal evidence and the actual rules of US programs, with the normative questions (Rawls, Sen, Nozick) kept in view; the text for the Wagner course. The Fiscal Policy Calculator scores tax and spending proposals the way its chapters describe.
  • Antitrust Research Methods Course-tested — a working manual that sets econometrics beside case law, qualitative evidence and agency practice across the US, EU and South Africa, with every figure reproducible in R; the text for the CCRED practitioner course at the University of Johannesburg.
  • Law and Economics In progress — economic theory set beside legal doctrine, empirical evidence and practice, for readers with intermediate micro and no legal training.

Political economy and development

  • Economic Coercion Revised full draft — sanctions, export controls, investment screening and tariffs as both law and economics: the statutes and agencies that make each tool work, and the evidence on what it costs and whom it hits, in a field that otherwise has journalism and law reviews but few textbooks; companion to EconWar.
  • Political Economy Full draft — how political institutions shape economic outcomes and economic conditions shape political behavior; a comparative field text for second-year PhD students.
  • Development Economics Full draft — bridges the micro-experimental and macro-structural traditions of the field.
  • The New Population Panic Revised full draft — falling fertility as a manageable transition rather than a catastrophe: the facts are right, the alarm rests on contested philosophy.

Economies, from city to continent

  • City Economics Full draft — how cities work, economically, socially and politically, with attention to history, policy and the lived experience of urban life.
  • Regional Economics In progress — six world regions through new economic geography, institutional analysis and spatial econometrics, each section paired with an applied data lab.
  • The American Economy In progress — how each sector of the US economy actually works: who the firms are, how they are regulated, where the workers are and what the data measure, for readers who find that macro aggregates hide what they most want to know.
  • The Chinese Economy Full draft — the same portrait for China, with the historical depth its transformation demands.
  • The South African Economy Revised full draft — the democratic era from 1994 to 2025 in three parts: macroeconomics and state capacity, the productive sectors, and labour, skills and distribution. Read it alongside SA Policy Space, which tracks the reform agenda the book describes.
  • The African Economy: The Growth Century First edition — the third in the country series and the first published as a website-textbook: Africa as the growth economy of the rest of this century, organized around the systems that cross borders (corridors, currency zones, city-regions, value chains, payment rails) and regional growth poles, with nine data tools and 54 country cards; first edition September 2026. Bridging African Borders tracks the cross-border reforms the book argues for.

History and ideas

  • World Economic History Full draft — from prehistory to the present, pairing each era with how we know what we know: the papers, data and identification strategies behind the evidence.
  • An Introduction to Economic Thought Revised full draft — modern questions, historical answers: every idea students now learn as settled was once contested, and the book follows the contests.
  • Philosophy of Economics Full draft — rationality, welfare, causation and scientific method, for beginning PhD students and advanced undergraduates.
